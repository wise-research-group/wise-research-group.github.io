Facilities

Where the group tests: the large-scale LSU WISE open-jet facility, a small-scale open jet for section-model tests, a structural control laboratory with a shake table, and high-performance computing for large-eddy simulation.

The LSU WISE open-jet facility

A wall-free jet with a 4 m by 4 m test section that reproduces complete atmospheric boundary-layer turbulence at Reynolds numbers up to 2.3 × 106, depending on the test model. Its integral length scales are about ten times those of conventional small-scale testing, and its velocity spectra agree well with established formulations. The facility supports testing of buildings, solar arrays and other renewable energy installations, wind turbines, bridges, and power transmission lines. The open-jet setup also allows destructive testing at high wind speeds and makes it easy to add water spray for wind-driven rain studies. Select a photo to enlarge it.

How the facility was built

The open jet began as a Phase I prototype: a small 15-fan wind generator that Dr. Aly designed and built with his students, who presented its first results in 2014. Its atmospheric boundary-layer flow was validated with CFD and measurements (Aly and Gol-Zaroudi, Measurement, 2017). The large-scale facility followed, with a 2 × 2 array of large fans driving the 4 m by 4 m test section. As principal investigator, Dr. Aly led the eight-investigator team that established the large-scale facility (2016–2017), and he prepared the AutoCAD drawings of its components.

Laboratories and computing

Beyond the large open jet: section-model testing, shake-table experiments, and the computing behind the group’s simulations.

Small-scale open jet

The 15-fan Phase I generator also serves as a small-scale open jet for section models. A cylinder section model of a traffic signal mast arm, with a ring of 16 pressure taps and Cobra probes upstream and downstream, validated full-scale large-eddy simulation (Experimental Techniques, 2022), and parapet effects on a large industrial building were measured here (Advances in Aerodynamics, 2021).

Structural control laboratory

Shake-table experiments on small-scale building models, with a data acquisition system, four MR dampers, and a laser displacement sensor for smart-structure studies. Here the tuned pendulum pounding mass damper was verified: with a 1% mass ratio, it reduced acceleration and stayed effective over a broader range of frequencies than a conventional tuned mass damper (Soil Dynamics and Earthquake Engineering, 2023).

High-performance computing

Large-eddy simulations run on LSU’s high-performance computing clusters with ANSYS Fluent and OpenFOAM, and they train the group’s machine-learning models: a deep-learning eddy-viscosity model trained on LES improved RANS predictions of separated flow in about 6 minutes, against about 182 hours for LES (Journal of Applied Fluid Mechanics, 2024).

Before LSU, Dr. Aly tested in the Politecnico di Milano wind tunnel, commissioned the Wall of Wind at Florida International University, and ran offshore wind turbine experiments in Western University’s Boundary Layer Wind Tunnel Laboratory; see Projects.

Data for AI and simulation

Fast prediction tools, from CFD to machine learning, are only as accurate as the measurements used to train and validate them. Small-scale tests can understate the peak loads that damage roofs and cladding. The open jet measures those loads at large scale, and the results are checked against full-scale field data (Physics of Fluids, 2024). The group uses its measurements to validate its own simulations, such as the large-eddy simulation of a traffic signal mast arm (Experimental Techniques, 2022).

Results from the facility

Highlights are on the Research page; the full record is on Publications.

  • Peak pressures run 25–300% higher than in small-scale tests and agree closely with full-scale field data (Physics of Fluids, 2024).
  • Rooftop solar panels reduced total wind-induced roof forces by 45–63% (Sustainable and Resilient Infrastructure, 2023).
  • On a 1:50 high-rise model, mean cladding pressures matched conventional wind tunnels while peak pressures ran higher (Practice Periodical on Structural Design and Construction, 2022).
